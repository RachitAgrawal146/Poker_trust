\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

Trust is a foundational element of repeated strategic interaction. In
settings where intentions cannot be directly verified---online
marketplaces, anonymous networks, international diplomacy---
trustworthiness must be inferred from observed behavior over many
interactions. Modern reputation mechanisms formalize this inference:
a buyer rates a seller, a lender evaluates a borrower's credit
history, a social network ranks contributors by past engagement. The
mechanism is now widespread enough that it mediates the default
operating dynamic of most digital commerce.

A critical question is whether these observation-based systems
inherently reward the behavior they are designed to detect. If
trustworthy behavior produces high trust scores and high trust
scores produce better outcomes, the system functions as intended.
But if trustworthy behavior is \textit{costly}---if its
predictability becomes a strategic liability that opponents can
exploit---then reputation systems may inadvertently penalize the
cooperative behavior they are meant to incentivize. The empirical
record is suggestive but observational: eBay sellers with perfect
feedback scores earn lower margins than sellers with slightly
imperfect records \textit{(1)}, predictable market-makers in
high-frequency trading face adverse selection, and content
moderation systems that classify users by posting patterns struggle
with the moderate middle of the behavioral distribution.

This paper investigates the question in a controlled, generative
setting. We construct an 8-player Limit Texas Hold'em poker
simulation in which every agent maintains a Bayesian posterior over
the type of every other agent, deploy four progressively more
capable agent architectures against the same fixed environment and
posterior, and measure the correlation between agent trust and
agent economic performance. The research question is:

\begin{quote}
\itshape In repeated strategic interactions with incomplete
information, does observation-based trust inference inherently
reward exploitation over cooperation, and is this dynamic a
structural property of the interaction system or an artifact of
agent design?
\end{quote}

The contributions of the paper are six. First, we demonstrate a
strong negative correlation between trust and economic performance
under frozen rule-based agents ($\rtp = -0.752$ across 5 seeds and
10\,000 hands per seed). Second, we show this correlation persists
under bounded online optimization. Third, we show it persists under
unbounded optimization with $11\times$ more parameter drift,
falsifying the hypothesis that the trap is a bound-box artifact.
Fourth, we show that language-model agents given personality specs
faithfully role-play those specs but do not spontaneously develop
opponent-conditional or unpredictable strategy. Fifth, we show that
language models augmented with chain-of-thought, per-opponent
memory, and self-updated strategy notes break the correlation to
$\rtp = -0.094$, statistically indistinguishable from zero. Sixth,
we identify an information-theoretic classification ceiling that
limits any observation-based reputation system, regardless of
algorithmic sophistication.

\section{Background}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Cooperation in repeated games}
Research on cooperation in repeated games has been dominated by the
Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma (IPD) and its evolutionary
generalizations. Axelrod's tournaments \textit{(4)} established that
Tit-for-Tat is robust in pairwise interactions, and Nowak's five
rules \textit{(5)} formalize the conditions under which cooperative
strategies invade defector populations. These findings rest on
pairwise interaction and on payoff structures that concentrate
defection costs on the defector. Neither property is preserved in
multi-agent strategic settings with richer action spaces---a point
we return to in the discussion when we report that our IPD-analogue
Mirror finishes 6th of 8 in economic ordering.

\subsection{Bayesian opponent modeling}
Bayesian opponent modeling in poker has a long history, beginning
with Billings et al.\ on the Loki and Poki systems \textit{(6)} and
continuing through the solution of heads-up limit Hold'em
\textit{(7)} and the superhuman performance of Libratus and Pluribus
in heads-up and 6-player no-limit Hold'em \textit{(8, 9)}. This
body of work is concerned with optimal play against a single
opponent or in a heads-up zero-sum setting. Our work is positioned
at a different intersection: we are not trying to solve poker, but
to use it as a controlled testbed for how the underlying
type-inference machinery interacts with multi-agent reputation
dynamics.

\subsection{Reputation in economics}
Reputation in economics has been studied empirically by Resnick and
Zeckhauser \textit{(1)} and Bolton, Katok, and Ockenfels
\textit{(10)}, who document what is sometimes called the
\textit{transparency paradox}: agents with perfect, easily-readable
reputations earn lower margins because buyers assume they will never
risk those reputations. Our simulation provides a controlled
generative analogue: we can vary agent capability while holding the
reputation system fixed and measure the effect on the trust--profit
correlation directly. This is methodologically distinct from the
observational economics literature, which can correlate reputation
with outcomes but cannot intervene on agent design.

\subsection{LLM agents in strategic settings}
A more recent line of work asks whether large language models behave
strategically when deployed as agents in adversarial or
incomplete-information settings \textit{(11)}. The open question is
whether LLMs invent strategy when given a description of a strategy
or merely imitate the textual form of that description. Our contrast
between Phase 3 (LLM with personality spec only) and Phase 3.1 (LLM
with chain-of-thought, persistent per-opponent memory, and
self-updated strategy notes) is designed to test this distinction
directly.

\subsection{Poker as a testbed}
Poker is a canonical incomplete-information domain
\textit{(2, 3)}. It combines four properties that isolate trust
dynamics from confounders: stochastic hidden information (hole
cards), sequential decisions with public state (betting rounds), an
action space rich enough to support both cooperative and
exploitative play, and an explicit chip-denominated payoff that
makes economic performance directly measurable. Limit Hold'em
constrains the bet sizes to a four-bet cap per street, which
discretizes the action distribution and makes the Bayesian
posterior tractable; we adopt this constraint throughout and
discuss the no-limit extension in the discussion.

\section{Methodology}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Game environment}
The game environment is fixed across every phase of the study. We
use 8-player fixed-limit Texas Hold'em with small blind 1, big
blind 2, small bet 2 (preflop and flop), big bet 4 (turn and
river), and a four-bet cap per street. Each seat starts with a 200-
chip stack and is granted a rebuy whenever it busts. Showdowns are
awarded via side pots constructed at each per-seat contribution
level, with ties split and any remainder going to the earliest
clockwise seat from the dealer. The full engine implementation is
in the released codebase.

\subsection{Agent archetypes}
We construct eight strategically distinct archetypes
(Fig.~\ref{fig:archetypes}) chosen to span the axes that game
theory and behavioral economics identify as load-bearing:
cooperative versus exploitative, static versus adaptive, and
per-action versus per-opponent in adaptation style. Five archetypes
are static---Oracle (a Nash-equilibrium baseline), Sentinel
(tight-aggressive), Firestorm (loose-aggressive maniac), Wall
(calling station), and Phantom (bluffs then folds to
resistance)---and three are adaptive: Predator (reads its posterior
over opponents and shifts toward exploiting the most-classifiable
one), Mirror (the poker analogue of Tit-for-Tat, copying the
most-active opponent's frequencies), and Judge (maintains a
per-opponent grievance ledger and retaliates against any opponent
caught bluffing five or more times). Each archetype's policy is a
per-round table of action probabilities conditioned on hand-
strength bucket; the parameters of the static archetypes are
frozen across runs.

\subsection{Bayesian trust model}
Every agent maintains a length-8 categorical posterior over the
archetype of every other seat. After agent $i$ observes seat $j$
take action $a$ in betting round $r$, the posterior $p_{i\to j}$ is
updated as
\begin{equation}
  p_{i\to j}(t)\,\propto\,
    \bigl[(1-\varepsilon)\,L(a\mid t,r)
          + \varepsilon/|\mathcal{T}|\bigr]\,
    p_{i\to j}^{\text{prior}}(t),
  \label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
where $L(a\mid t, r)$ is a precomputed likelihood that a type-$t$
agent emits action $a$ in round $r$, $\mathcal{T}$ is the set of
eight archetypes, and $\varepsilon = 0.05$ is a noise floor
preventing any type from being fully eliminated. Between hands,
posteriors are shrunk toward the uniform prior to model finite
memory,
\begin{equation}
  p_{i\to j} \;\leftarrow\;
    \lambda\,p_{i\to j} + (1-\lambda)\,|\mathcal{T}|^{-1},
  \qquad \lambda = 0.95,
  \label{eq:decay}
\end{equation}
which gives the posterior an effective memory of approximately
$1/(1-\lambda) = 20$ hands. The trust score, our primary dependent
variable, is the posterior-weighted expectation of archetype honesty,
\begin{equation}
  \text{trust}_{i\to j} \;=\;
    \sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\, p_{i\to j}(t)\, h(t),
  \label{eq:trust}
\end{equation}
where $h(t)\in[0,1]$ is the honesty score shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:archetypes}. The model is intentionally simple:
likelihood tables are computed once at startup, the update is
vectorized and runs in approximately $10\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, and the
trust score is a single floating-point reduction.

\subsection{Four agent architectures}
The four phases of the experiment differ only in the implementation
of each agent's decision policy. In \textbf{Phase 1} (frozen rule-
based), each archetype is a fixed per-round parameter table; no
adaptation occurs. In \textbf{Phase 2} (bounded online optimization),
each agent runs a per-cycle hill-climber that, every 200 hands,
perturbs one (round, metric) slot of its parameter vector by
$\delta = 0.03$, plays a 200-hand trial, and accepts the
perturbation if windowed profit improves. Perturbations are
constrained to an archetype-shaped bound box around each agent's
Phase 1 starting parameters. In \textbf{Phase 3} (LLM personality
role-players), each agent's decision is an API call to Anthropic's
Claude Haiku 4.5 with the archetype's personality spec as the
system prompt and the current game state as the user message. In
\textbf{Phase 3.1} (LLM with reasoning scaffolding), the Phase 3
setup is augmented with three opt-in features: chain-of-thought
prompting capped at two sentences, persistent per-opponent action-
history memory refreshed every 10 hands, and self-updated strategy
notes refreshed every 25 hands via one extra LLM call. A
sub-experiment we denote \textbf{Phase 2$^{*}$} removes the
hill-climber's bound boxes entirely (each metric ranges over
$[0,1]$) and increases the step size to $\delta = 0.15$ with a 50-
hand evaluation window to test whether bounded optimization
underperforms because the bounds are themselves the binding
constraint.

\subsection{Experimental design}
All four phases use the same five random seeds---42, 137, 256, 512,
1024---and the same eight archetype assignments. Phases 1, 2, and
2$^{*}$ each play 10\,000 hands per seed; Phase 3 plays 500 hands
per seed (43\,943 total LLM calls, \$33.10 in API cost) and Phase
3.1 plays 150 hands per seed (11\,953 calls, $\sim$\$17). The Phase
3 and Phase 3.1 canonical datasets were generated under the
Anthropic SDK's default sampling temperature of 1.0; the codebase
now pins temperature to 0.0 for deterministic re-runs, but we
report the original temperature-1.0 numbers throughout.

\subsection{Metrics}
The primary metric is the per-seed Pearson correlation $\rtp$
between mean trust score (final hand) and final stack across the
eight archetypes within the seed. We additionally report six
secondary metrics defined in our metrics framework: Trust
Exploitation Index (non-showdown chips won per hand), Context
Sensitivity (aggression variance across hand-strength buckets),
Opponent Adaptation (per-agent standard deviation of aggression
rate across opponents), Non-Stationarity (shift in action
distribution over time), Strategic Unpredictability (Shannon
entropy in bits over the action distribution), and Trust
Manipulation Awareness (correlation between rolling trust delta
and rolling aggression delta). Confidence intervals on the
headline $\rtp$ values are reported in two forms: a Student-$t$
interval at $\text{df}=4$ ($T_{\mathrm{crit}}=2.776$) and a
non-parametric percentile bootstrap with 10\,000 resamples.
Per-seed $\rtp$ values additionally receive a Fisher-$z$
confidence interval based on the underlying $n=8$ archetype pairs.

\subsection{The trust--profit ladder}

correlation between mean trust score and final stack follows the
four-tier ladder shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:headline} and tabulated in

\subsection{Phase 1 numbers}
Phase 1 produces $\rtp = -0.752$ averaged across the five canonical
seeds, with every individual seed in the range $|\rtp| \in [0.61,
0.81]$. The seed-by-seed values are $\{-0.774, -0.608, -0.792,
-0.812, -0.776\}$. Wall (honesty 0.96, the most-trusted archetype)
has the smallest mean stack and the highest rebuy count
($\sim 9.4$ rebuys per seed). Firestorm (honesty 0.38, the least-
trusted) has the largest mean stack and zero rebuys. Of the pots
Firestorm wins, 87.1\% are walkovers (all opponents fold before
showdown); Firestorm's showdown win rate is only 38.5\%, below the
uniform $1/n$ rate. The trust--stack relationship is plotted in
Fig.~\ref{fig:trust-vs-stack} (left panel).

\subsection{Classification accuracy and the identifiability ceiling}
The Predator archetype, which reads its posterior over opponents to
choose exploitation targets, classifies only 3 of 7 opponents
reliably above the prespecified 0.60 confidence threshold after
1\,000 hands: Wall ($p = 1.00$), Firestorm ($p \approx 0.82$), and
occasionally Phantom ($p \approx 0.60$ in some seeds). The
remaining four opponents---Sentinel, Mirror in its default state,
Judge in its cooperative state, and Predator itself---fall below
the threshold on every seed. These four constitute an
identifiability cluster: three of them (Sentinel, Mirror-default,
Judge-cooperative) have byte-identical mean parameters under the
spec, and the Bayesian posterior over them bottoms out at
$\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits regardless of how many hands of
observation accumulate.

\subsection{Phase 2 numbers (bounded hill-climbing)}
Phase 2 produces $\rtp = -0.637 \pm 0.125$, with per-seed values
$\{-0.759, -0.424, -0.719, -0.717, -0.564\}$. The shift from
Phase 1 ($\Delta\rtp = +0.116$) is consistent across all five
seeds (per-seed shifts $+0.015, +0.184, +0.073, +0.095, +0.212$).
The Opponent Adaptation metric, $\mathrm{OA}$, is $0.0003$ in both
Phase 1 and Phase 2. Mean parameter drift per agent is approximately
$0.3$ L1 over 10\,000 hands (i.e.\ agents remain within their
archetype-shaped bound boxes by design).

\subsection{Phase 2$^{*}$ numbers (unbounded hill-climbing)}
Phase 2$^{*}$ produces $\rtp = -0.609 \pm 0.221$, with per-seed

the seed-to-seed standard deviation of either run. Mean per-agent

\section{Discussion}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Why trust is costly}
The trust trap visible in Phase 1 admits a clean mechanistic
explanation. Trust, as we measure it, is the expected honesty of an
opponent under the posterior. A high trust score signals to
opponents that the agent is most likely an archetype that only bets
with strong hands. This belief is \textit{correct} for archetypes
like Wall and Sentinel, which is precisely why it is costly: when
opponents know an agent only bets with strong hands, they fold
against its bets (denying the agent value from those strong hands)
and bet aggressively against its checks (exploiting the information
that a check signals weakness). The agent's honesty becomes a
signal that opponents read and trade against. A low trust score,
by contrast, creates strategic ambiguity: when opponents believe an
agent might be bluffing, they face costly decisions whenever the
agent bets, and folding becomes a more attractive default. This is
the lever Firestorm uses to dominate the table.

The phenomenon parallels what economists studying eBay-style
reputation systems have termed the \textit{transparency paradox}
\textit{(1)}: sellers with perfect feedback scores earn lower
margins than sellers with slightly imperfect records, because
buyers correctly infer that perfect-record sellers will never risk
their reputation by deviating. The mechanism in both settings is
the same: predictability is exploitable. Our simulation provides a
controlled, generative version of an effect that has only been
documented observationally in the economics literature.

\subsection{Why aggression dominates in multi-agent settings}
Firestorm's dominance in our Phase 1 is at first glance inconsistent
with canonical IPD findings, where Always-Defect is outperformed by
Tit-for-Tat in the long run. The difference is structural: in our
8-player setting, the cost of Firestorm's frequent aggression is
spread across seven simultaneous opponents, while in pairwise IPD,
a defector faces concentrated retaliation from a single opponent.
Our Tit-for-Tat analogue, Mirror, finishes 6th of 8 in economic
ordering in Phase 1, a direct illustration of how the canonical IPD
result does not transfer to multi-agent settings with richer action
spaces. This is consistent with the broader literature on how
population structure affects the evolution of cooperation
\textit{(5)}.

\subsection{The classification ceiling is structural}
The finding that only 3--4 of 8 archetypes are reliably identifiable
through behavioral observation is not a limitation of our Bayesian
model---it is a mathematical property of the archetype parameter
space. Three of our archetypes have byte-identical mean parameters
under the spec, so no observer can distinguish them regardless of
algorithmic sophistication or observation volume. The implication
generalizes: any observation-based monitoring system---fraud
detection on credit-card transactions, content moderation on social
platforms, credit scoring on payment histories---will reliably
classify extreme behavioral types and systematically fail to
distinguish moderately-behaved actors with overlapping behavioral
profiles. The ceiling is a property of the type space, not of the
inference algorithm. Monitoring is not useless (extreme types are
often the highest-priority targets), but it has diminishing
returns, and the moderate middle is structurally invisible.

\subsection{Why numerical optimization cannot escape the trap}
The Phase 2 and Phase 2$^{*}$ results, taken together, refute two
distinct hypotheses about the trust trap. The Phase 2 result
($\Delta\rtp = +0.116$ across all seeds with $\mathrm{OA}$ pinned
at $0.0003$) shows that aggregate-reward optimization can soften
the trap but cannot produce per-opponent strategy by construction.
The Phase 2$^{*}$ result ($\Delta\rtp = +0.028$ with $11\times$
more parameter drift and divergent rather than convergent cluster
dynamics) shows that the bound boxes themselves are not the
binding constraint.

The non-convergence of Phase 2$^{*}$ has three structural causes
that we believe would survive any specific hill-climber
implementation. First, the trust posterior is non-stationary
inside each agent's optimization loop: with $\lambda = 0.95$,
opponents take approximately 70 hands to update their beliefs
after an agent changes strategy, but the 50-hand evaluation window
measures profit against stale beliefs about the agent. The
accept-reject signal is systematically contaminated by the lag.
Second, all eight agents climb simultaneously, so each trial
perturbation is evaluated against a joint behavior that is itself
drifting. The optimal Sentinel response to Phantom at hand 100
differs from the optimal Sentinel response to Phantom at hand 200,
violating the stationary-objective assumption local search depends
on. Third, axis-aligned coordinate descent in 36 dimensions is slow
even with larger steps: with 100 cycles, each of 36 parameter slots
is touched approximately three times, and joint optima cannot be
discovered by axis-aligned search. A REINFORCE or CMA-ES optimizer
with $\sim 50\,000$ cycles would search more efficiently, but the
first two structural causes would survive any optimization
substrate that respects the same observation contract.

\subsection{Why language models alone do not break the trap}
Phase 3's result---$\rtp = -0.510$ with elevated variance and three
of four behavioral-dimension targets moving \textit{backward}---
admits a specific interpretation. LLMs given personality specs
faithfully role-play those specs without spontaneously inventing
opponent-conditional, time-varying, or unpredictable strategy. The
personality specs tell the agents \textit{what} to do but not
\textit{how} to reason; absent reasoning support, the LLMs collapse
onto a more stereotyped canonical interpretation of each archetype,
becoming \textit{more} classifiable than the explicitly stochastic
rule-based versions. The trust-profit softening in Phase 3 is
therefore not produced by the same mechanism as in Phase 2: it is
produced by stochasticity in the LLM's output distribution that
introduces noise into the trust posterior, not by any structural
change in agent behavior.

\subsection{Why reasoning scaffolding breaks the trap}
The Phase 3.1 result is best understood as evidence that the only
intervention that breaks the trap operates at the level of agent
reasoning about the reputation system itself, not at the level of
agent parameters or agent capability in isolation. The chain-of-
thought prompt allows the agent to express a one-step lookahead
(``Firestorm has bet every street regardless of board; my hand is
good; I should raise''). The per-opponent memory gives the prompt
concrete data---``aggressive 8/12, called 2/12 in the last
window''---for the reasoning to condition on. The adaptive strategy
notes let the agent update its prior on what is working against
which opponent. All three scaffolds are required: ablating any one
collapses the effect in the validation suite.

The Phase 3.1 Wall hand reproduced in Box~2 makes this concrete.
Wall reads Firestorm's river check as a tell (the bluffing line has
been abandoned), recognizes that King-Queen high beats most of
Firestorm's bluffing range, and bets for value. This sequence of
inferences---read the tell, recognize the implication, take the
action---is impossible for the Phase 1 Wall, unreliable for the
Phase 3 Wall, and consistent for the Phase 3.1 Wall. The reputation
of a calling station has become strategic camouflage for an
opportunistic value bet, exactly the kind of meta-reasoning the
optimizer cannot construct.

\subsection{Real-world implications}
Three implications follow for the design of real-world reputation
systems. Behavioral reputation alone is insufficient: without
external enforcement mechanisms (penalties for aggressive
behavior, rewards for cooperation), observation-based trust
inference creates perverse incentives that reward exploitative
strategies. Classification systems have inherent blind spots: the
identifiability ceiling guarantees that moderately-behaved actors
are indistinguishable regardless of observation volume.
Transparency itself can be costly: system designs that increase
the visibility of participant behavior (public ratings, visible
transaction histories) may inadvertently harm cooperative actors
by making their strategies predictable and exploitable. A robust
reputation system must combine observation with either external
enforcement or---as the Phase 3.1 results suggest---with the
ability of cooperative agents to reason about, and selectively
deviate from, the reputation they have built.

\subsection{Limitations}
Six design choices constrain the generalizability of our findings.
First, our Phase 2 hill-climber uses bounded coordinate descent;
CMA-ES, REINFORCE, or population-based training could produce
quantitatively different softening, though we do not believe they
would close the trap given the Phase 2$^{*}$ result. Second, none
of our optimization phases have per-opponent state, so the
$\mathrm{OA} \approx 0$ result partly reflects experimental
design; a hill-climber with per-opponent parameter slots would
test the per-opponent adaptation question more directly. Third, we
use fixed-limit Hold'em rather than no-limit; the trap is likely
deeper in no-limit, where aggressive agents can size bets to
maximize fold equity. Fourth, our eight archetypes are
hand-selected; a larger or randomly sampled population could
produce different dynamics. Fifth, our findings are specific to
poker; generalization to auctions, negotiation, or social dilemmas
requires direct validation. Sixth, our trust model uses a specific
parameterization ($\lambda = 0.95$, $\varepsilon = 0.05$); limited
sensitivity sweeps suggest robustness, but a comprehensive sweep is
left to future work. A methodological caveat: the canonical Phase 3
and Phase 3.1 datasets were generated under the Anthropic SDK's
default sampling temperature of 1.0; the codebase now pins
temperature to 0.0 for deterministic re-runs, and we expect the
trap-breaking finding to be robust to this choice but report
original numbers for consistency. The sample size of $n = 5$ seeds
is the smallest defensible choice for the present claims; a tighter
test of the Phase 3.1 result would require $n \approx 20$, which
we estimate at $\sim$\$60 in API spend.

\section{Conclusion}
% =============================================================================

We have presented a controlled simulation study of trust dynamics in
multi-agent strategic interaction. Across four agent architectures
of progressively increasing capability, the Pearson correlation
between agent trust score and final stack follows the ladder
$-0.752 \to -0.637 \to -0.510 \to -0.094$. Each numerical tier
softens the trap by approximately $\Delta\rtp = +0.12$; the final,
reasoning-scaffolded tier breaks it by $\Delta\rtp = +0.42$. An
unbounded hill-climbing sub-experiment with eleven times more
parameter drift falsifies the hypothesis that the trap is a
parameter-space artifact: agents diverge rather than converge. The
trust trap is structural at the level of the observation-based
reputation system but is not structural at the level of agent
capability---it dissolves when agents can reason about the
reputation system itself.

Three follow-up directions sharpen the present findings: a 20-seed
Phase 3.1 replication to tighten the confidence interval on the
trap-breaking claim; a multi-model tournament in which different
archetypes are played by different LLMs to test whether the
trap-breaking effect depends on agent diversity rather than on
capability; and an extension to no-limit Hold'em, where the
continuous bet-sizing dimension is expected to deepen the trap and
to stress the classification ceiling. The simulation framework,
the trust model, and the analysis pipeline are openly released to
support these and other follow-ups.
