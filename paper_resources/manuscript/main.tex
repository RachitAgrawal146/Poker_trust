\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

Trust is a foundational element of repeated strategic interaction. In
settings where intentions cannot be directly verified---online
marketplaces, anonymous networks, international diplomacy---
trustworthiness must be inferred from observed behavior over many
interactions. Modern reputation mechanisms formalize this inference:
a buyer rates a seller, a lender evaluates a borrower's credit
history, a social network ranks contributors by past engagement. The
mechanism now mediates the default operating dynamic of most digital
commerce.

A critical question is whether these observation-based systems
inherently reward the behavior they are designed to detect. If
trustworthy behavior produces high trust scores and high trust
scores produce better outcomes, the system functions as intended.
But if trustworthy behavior is \textit{costly}---if its
predictability becomes a strategic liability that opponents can
exploit---then reputation systems may inadvertently penalize the
cooperative behavior they are meant to incentivize. The empirical
record is suggestive but observational: eBay sellers with perfect
feedback scores earn lower margins than sellers with slightly
imperfect records \textit{(1)}, predictable market-makers face
adverse selection, and content moderation systems struggle with the
moderate middle of the behavioral distribution.

This paper investigates the question in a controlled, generative
setting. We construct an 8-player Limit Texas Hold'em simulation in
which every agent maintains a Bayesian posterior over the type of
every other agent, deploy four progressively more capable agent
architectures against the same fixed environment, and measure the
correlation between trust and economic performance.

The research question is whether, in repeated strategic
interactions with incomplete information, observation-based trust
inference inherently rewards exploitation over cooperation, and
whether the dynamic is a structural property of the interaction
system or an artifact of agent design.

The contributions of the paper are six. \textit{First}, we
demonstrate a strong negative correlation between trust and
economic performance under frozen rule-based agents
($\rtp = -0.752$). \textit{Second}, the correlation persists under
bounded online optimization. \textit{Third}, it persists under
unbounded optimization with $11\times$ more parameter drift,
falsifying the hypothesis that the trap is a bound-box artifact.
\textit{Fourth}, language-model agents given personality specs
faithfully role-play those specs but do not spontaneously develop
opponent-conditional or unpredictable strategy. \textit{Fifth},
language models augmented with chain-of-thought, per-opponent
memory, and self-updated strategy notes break the correlation to
$\rtp = -0.094$, statistically indistinguishable from zero.
\textit{Sixth}, we identify an information-theoretic classification
ceiling that limits any observation-based reputation system.

\section{Background}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Cooperation in repeated games}
Research on cooperation in repeated games has been dominated by the
Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma (IPD) and its evolutionary
generalizations. Axelrod's tournaments \textit{(4)} established that
Tit-for-Tat is robust in pairwise interactions, and Nowak's five
rules \textit{(5)} formalize the conditions under which cooperative
strategies invade defector populations. These findings rest on
pairwise interaction and on payoff structures that concentrate
defection costs on the defector. Neither property is preserved in
multi-agent settings with richer action spaces---a point we return
to in the discussion when we report that our Tit-for-Tat analogue
(\arch{Mirror}) finishes sixth of eight in economic ordering.

\subsection{Bayesian opponent modeling}
Bayesian opponent modeling in poker has a long history, beginning
with Billings et al.\ on the Loki and Poki systems \textit{(6)} and
continuing through the solution of heads-up limit Hold'em
\textit{(7)} and the superhuman performance of Libratus and Pluribus
in no-limit Hold'em \textit{(8, 9)}. This body of work is concerned
with optimal play against a single opponent or in heads-up zero-sum
settings. Our work uses poker as a controlled testbed for how the
underlying type-inference machinery interacts with multi-agent
reputation dynamics, not as a benchmark to solve.

\subsection{Reputation in economics}
Reputation in economics has been studied empirically by Resnick and
Zeckhauser \textit{(1)} and Bolton, Katok, and Ockenfels
\textit{(10)}, who document what is sometimes called the
\textit{transparency paradox}: agents with perfect, easily-readable
reputations earn lower margins because buyers correctly infer that
they will never risk those reputations. Our simulation provides a
controlled generative analogue of this empirical phenomenon.

\subsection{LLM agents in strategic settings}
A more recent line of work asks whether large language models behave
strategically when deployed as agents in adversarial settings
\textit{(11)}. The open question is whether LLMs invent strategy
when given a description of strategy or merely imitate the textual
form of that description. Our contrast between Phase 3 (LLM with
personality spec only) and Phase 3.1 (LLM with reasoning
scaffolding) is designed to test this distinction.

\subsection{Poker as a testbed}
Poker is a canonical incomplete-information domain \textit{(2, 3)}
combining four properties that isolate trust dynamics from
confounders: stochastic hidden information, sequential decisions
with public state, a rich action space, and explicit chip-
denominated payoffs. Limit Hold'em constrains the bet sizes to a
four-bet cap per street, discretizing the action distribution and
keeping the Bayesian posterior tractable.

\section{Methodology}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Game environment}
The game environment is fixed across every phase of the study. We
use 8-player fixed-limit Texas Hold'em with small blind 1, big
blind 2, small bet 2 (preflop and flop), big bet 4 (turn and
river), and a four-bet cap per street. Each seat starts with a 200-
chip stack and is granted a rebuy whenever it busts. Showdowns are
awarded via side pots constructed at each per-seat contribution
level, with ties split and any remainder going to the earliest
clockwise seat from the dealer.

\subsection{The eight agent archetypes}
We construct eight strategically distinct archetypes spanning the
axes that game theory and behavioral economics identify as
load-bearing: cooperative versus exploitative, static versus
adaptive, per-action versus per-opponent in adaptation style. Each
agent's policy is a per-round table of action probabilities
conditioned on hand-strength bucket; the parameters of the static
archetypes are frozen across runs. Each archetype carries an
\textit{honesty score} $h \in [0,1]$ defined as
$P(\text{bet}\mid\text{strong})\cdot(1-P(\text{bluff}\mid\text{weak}))$;
this scalar is what the trust score weights
(Eq.~\ref{eq:trust}).

\subsection{Bayesian trust model}
Every agent maintains a length-8 categorical posterior over the
archetype of every other seat. After agent $i$ observes seat $j$
take action $a$ in betting round $r$, the posterior is updated as
\begin{equation}
  p_{i\to j}(t)\,\propto\,
    \bigl[(1-\varepsilon)\,L(a\mid t,r)
          + \varepsilon/|\mathcal{T}|\bigr]\,
    p_{i\to j}^{\text{prior}}(t),
  \label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
where $L(a\mid t, r)$ is a precomputed likelihood that a type-$t$
agent emits action $a$ in round $r$, $\mathcal{T}$ is the set of
eight archetypes, and $\varepsilon = 0.05$ is a noise floor
preventing any type from being fully eliminated. Between hands,
posteriors are shrunk toward the uniform prior:
\begin{equation}
  p_{i\to j} \;\leftarrow\;
    \lambda\,p_{i\to j} + (1-\lambda)\,|\mathcal{T}|^{-1},
  \qquad \lambda = 0.95,
  \label{eq:decay}
\end{equation}
giving an effective memory of approximately $1/(1-\lambda) = 20$
hands. The trust score, our primary dependent variable, is
\begin{equation}
  \text{trust}_{i\to j} \;=\;
    \sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\, p_{i\to j}(t)\, h(t),
  \label{eq:trust}
\end{equation}
where $h(t)\in[0,1]$ is the archetype's honesty score.

\subsection{Four agent architectures}
The four phases differ only in the implementation of each agent's
decision policy. \textbf{Phase 1} (frozen rule-based): each
archetype is a fixed per-round parameter table. \textbf{Phase 2}
(bounded online optimization): each agent runs a per-cycle hill-
climber that, every 200 hands, perturbs one (round, metric) slot by
$\delta = 0.03$, plays a 200-hand trial, and accepts the
perturbation if windowed profit improves; perturbations are
confined to an archetype-shaped bound box. \textbf{Phase 3} (LLM
personality role-players): each agent's decision is an API call to
Anthropic's Claude Haiku 4.5 with the archetype's personality spec
as the system prompt. \textbf{Phase 3.1} (LLM with reasoning
scaffolding): the Phase 3 setup augmented with chain-of-thought
prompting capped at two sentences, persistent per-opponent memory
refreshed every 10 hands, and self-updated strategy notes refreshed
every 25 hands. A sub-experiment \textbf{Phase 2$^{*}$} removes the
hill-climber's bound boxes entirely and raises the step size to
$\delta = 0.15$ with a 50-hand evaluation window.

\subsection{Experimental design}
All four phases use the same five random seeds (42, 137, 256, 512,
1024) and the same eight archetype assignments. Phases 1, 2, and
2$^{*}$ each play 10\,000 hands per seed. Phase 3 plays 500 hands
per seed (43\,943 total LLM calls, \$33.10). Phase 3.1 plays 150
hands per seed (11\,953 calls, $\sim$\$17). The Phase 3 and Phase
3.1 canonical datasets were generated under the Anthropic SDK's
default sampling temperature of 1.0; the codebase now pins
temperature to 0.0 for deterministic re-runs.

\subsection{Metrics}
The primary metric is the per-seed Pearson correlation $\rtp$
between mean trust score (final hand) and final stack across the
eight archetypes. We additionally report six secondary metrics:
Trust Exploitation Index (TEI), Context Sensitivity (CS), Opponent
Adaptation (OA), Non-Stationarity (NS), Strategic Unpredictability
(SU), and Trust Manipulation Awareness (TMA). Confidence intervals
on headline $\rtp$ values are reported in two forms: a Student-$t$
interval at $\text{df}=4$ and a non-parametric percentile bootstrap
with 10\,000 resamples.

\section{Results}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{The four-tier ladder}
Across the four canonical agent architectures, the Pearson
correlation between trust and stack follows the four-tier ladder of
Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}, tabulated in Table~1. Phase 1 produces
$\rtp = -0.752 \pm 0.073$; Phase 2 softens to $-0.637 \pm 0.125$;
Phase 2$^{*}$ to $-0.609 \pm 0.221$; Phase 3 to $-0.510 \pm 0.268$;
Phase 3.1 to $-0.094 \pm 0.301$. The 95\% Student-$t$ interval on
the Phase 3.1 mean is $[-0.512, +0.323]$; the bootstrap interval is
$[-0.324, +0.203]$. Both contain zero.

\subsection{Phase 1 numbers}
Phase 1 produces $\rtp = -0.752$ averaged across the five canonical
seeds, with per-seed values $\{-0.774, -0.608, -0.792, -0.812,
-0.776\}$. \arch{Wall} (the most-trusted archetype, honesty 0.96)
has the smallest mean stack and approximately 9.4 rebuys per seed.
\arch{Firestorm} (the least-trusted, honesty 0.38) has the largest
mean stack and zero rebuys. Of the pots \arch{Firestorm} wins,
$87.1\%$ are walkovers in which all opponents fold before showdown;
\arch{Firestorm}'s showdown win rate is only $38.5\%$, below the
uniform $1/n$ rate. The trust--stack relationship is shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:trust-vs-stack}.

\subsection{Classification accuracy and the identifiability ceiling}
The \arch{Predator} archetype, which reads its posterior to choose
exploitation targets, classifies only 3 of 7 opponents reliably
above the prespecified 0.60 confidence threshold after 1\,000
hands: \arch{Wall} ($p = 1.00$), \arch{Firestorm} ($p \approx
0.82$), and occasionally \arch{Phantom} ($p \approx 0.60$ in some
seeds). The remaining four opponents fall below the threshold on
every seed. Three of these (\arch{Sentinel}, \arch{Mirror} in its
default state, \arch{Judge} in its cooperative state) have byte-
identical mean parameters under the spec; the Bayesian posterior
over them bottoms out at $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits regardless
of observation volume.

\subsection{Phase 2 numbers (bounded hill-climbing)}
Phase 2 produces $\rtp = -0.637 \pm 0.125$ with per-seed values
$\{-0.759, -0.424, -0.719, -0.717, -0.564\}$. The shift from Phase
1 ($\Delta\rtp = +0.116$) is consistent across all five seeds
(per-seed shifts $+0.015, +0.184, +0.073, +0.095, +0.212$). The
Opponent Adaptation metric stays at $\mathrm{OA} = 0.0003$ in both
Phase 1 and Phase 2. Mean per-agent L1 drift in parameter space is
approximately 0.3 over 10\,000 hands.

\section{Discussion}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Why trust is costly}
The trust trap visible in Phase 1 admits a clean mechanistic
explanation. Trust, as we measure it, is the expected honesty of an
opponent under the posterior. A high trust score signals that the
agent is most likely an archetype that only bets with strong hands.
This belief is \textit{correct} for \arch{Wall} (honesty 0.96) and
\arch{Sentinel} (honesty 0.92), which is precisely why it is
costly: when opponents know an agent only bets with strong hands,
they fold against its bets (denying value from those strong hands)
and bet aggressively against its checks (exploiting the
information that a check signals weakness). The agent's honesty
becomes a signal that opponents read and trade against. A low trust
score creates strategic ambiguity that \arch{Firestorm} uses to
dominate the table. The phenomenon parallels what economists
studying eBay-style reputation systems have called the
\textit{transparency paradox} \textit{(1)}.

\subsection{Why aggression dominates in multi-agent settings}
\arch{Firestorm}'s dominance is at first glance inconsistent with
canonical IPD findings, where Always-Defect is outperformed by
Tit-for-Tat. The difference is structural: in our 8-player setting,
the cost of \arch{Firestorm}'s frequent aggression is spread across
seven simultaneous opponents, while in pairwise IPD a defector
faces concentrated retaliation. \arch{Mirror}, our Tit-for-Tat
analogue, finishes 6th of 8 in economic ordering---direct evidence
that the canonical IPD result does not transfer to multi-agent
settings with richer action spaces.

\subsection{The classification ceiling is structural}
The finding that only 3--4 of 8 archetypes are reliably identifiable
is not a limitation of our Bayesian model---it is a mathematical
property of the archetype parameter space. Three archetypes
(\arch{Sentinel}, \arch{Mirror}'s default, \arch{Judge}'s
cooperative state) have byte-identical mean parameters under the
spec; no observer can distinguish them regardless of algorithmic
sophistication. The implication generalizes: any observation-based
monitoring system---fraud detection, content moderation, credit
scoring---will reliably classify extreme behavioral types and fail
on the moderate middle. The ceiling is a property of the type
space, not the inference algorithm.

\subsection{Why numerical optimization cannot escape the trap}
The Phase 2 and Phase 2$^{*}$ results, taken together, refute two
distinct hypotheses about the trap. The Phase 2 result
($\Delta\rtp = +0.116$ with $\mathrm{OA}$ pinned at $0.0003$) shows
that aggregate-reward optimization softens the trap but cannot
produce per-opponent strategy by construction. The Phase 2$^{*}$
result ($\Delta\rtp = +0.028$ with $11\times$ more parameter drift
and divergent rather than convergent cluster dynamics) shows that
the bound boxes are not the binding constraint.

The non-convergence of Phase 2$^{*}$ has three structural causes.
\textit{First}, the trust posterior is non-stationary inside each
agent's optimization loop: with $\lambda = 0.95$, opponents take
$\sim$70 hands to update their beliefs after an agent changes
strategy, but the 50-hand evaluation window measures profit against
\textit{stale} beliefs about the agent. The accept-reject signal is
systematically contaminated by the lag. \textit{Second}, all eight
agents climb simultaneously; each trial perturbation is evaluated
against a joint behavior that is itself drifting, which violates the
stationary-objective assumption local search depends on.
\textit{Third}, axis-aligned coordinate descent in 36 dimensions is
slow even with larger steps: joint optima---``raise more \textit{and}
bluff more proportionally''---cannot be discovered by axis-aligned
search.

\subsection{Why language models alone do not break the trap}
Phase 3's result admits a specific interpretation. LLMs given
personality specs faithfully role-play those specs without
spontaneously inventing opponent-conditional, time-varying, or
unpredictable strategy. The personality specs tell the agents
\textit{what} to do but not \textit{how} to reason; absent
reasoning support, the LLMs collapse onto a more stereotyped
canonical interpretation of each archetype, becoming \textit{more}
classifiable than the explicitly stochastic rule-based versions.

\subsection{Why reasoning scaffolding breaks the trap}
The Phase 3.1 result is evidence that the only intervention that
breaks the trap operates at the level of agent reasoning about the
reputation system itself. The chain-of-thought prompt allows the
agent to express a one-step lookahead. The per-opponent memory
gives that reasoning concrete data to condition on. The adaptive
strategy notes let the agent update its prior on what is working
against which opponent. All three scaffolds are required---ablating
any one collapses the effect. The \arch{Wall} hand reproduced in
Box~2 makes this concrete: \arch{Wall} reads \arch{Firestorm}'s
river check as a tell, recognizes that K-Q high beats
\arch{Firestorm}'s bluffing range, and bets for value. This
sequence is impossible for Phase 1 \arch{Wall}, unreliable for
Phase 3 \arch{Wall}, and consistent for Phase 3.1 \arch{Wall}.

\subsection{Real-world implications}
Three implications follow for the design of real-world reputation
systems. \textit{Behavioral reputation alone is insufficient}:
without external enforcement mechanisms, observation-based trust
inference creates perverse incentives that reward exploitative
strategies. \textit{Classification systems have inherent blind
spots}: the identifiability ceiling guarantees that moderately-
behaved actors are indistinguishable regardless of observation
volume. \textit{Transparency itself can be costly}: system designs
that increase the visibility of participant behavior may
inadvertently harm cooperative actors by making their strategies
predictable and exploitable.

\subsection{Limitations}
Six design choices constrain generalizability. \textit{First}, our
Phase 2 hill-climber uses bounded coordinate descent; CMA-ES,
REINFORCE, or population-based training could produce different
quantitative softening, though we do not believe they would close
the trap given the Phase 2$^{*}$ result. \textit{Second}, none of
our optimization phases have per-opponent state. \textit{Third}, we
use fixed-limit Hold'em rather than no-limit; the trap is likely
deeper in no-limit. \textit{Fourth}, our eight archetypes are
hand-selected. \textit{Fifth}, our findings are specific to poker.
\textit{Sixth}, our trust model uses a specific parameterization
($\lambda = 0.95$, $\varepsilon = 0.05$). A methodological caveat:
the canonical Phase 3 and 3.1 datasets were generated under SDK
default temperature (1.0); the code now pins temperature 0.0. The
sample size of $n = 5$ seeds is the smallest defensible choice;
$n \approx 20$ would tighten the Phase 3.1 confidence interval at
an estimated cost of $\sim$\$60 in API spend.

\section{Conclusion}
% =============================================================================

We have presented a controlled simulation study of trust dynamics in
multi-agent strategic interaction. Across four agent architectures
of progressively increasing capability, the Pearson correlation
between agent trust score and final stack follows the ladder
$-0.752 \to -0.637 \to -0.510 \to -0.094$. Each numerical tier
softens the trap by approximately $\Delta\rtp = +0.12$; the final,
reasoning-scaffolded tier breaks it by $\Delta\rtp = +0.42$. An
unbounded hill-climbing sub-experiment with eleven times more
parameter drift falsifies the hypothesis that the trap is a
parameter-space artifact: agents diverge rather than converge. The
trust trap is structural at the level of the observation-based
reputation system but is not structural at the level of agent
capability---it dissolves when agents can reason about the
reputation system itself.

Three follow-up directions sharpen the present findings: a 20-seed
Phase 3.1 replication to tighten the confidence interval on the
trap-breaking claim; a multi-model tournament in which different
archetypes are played by different LLMs to test whether the
trap-breaking effect depends on agent diversity rather than on
capability; and an extension to no-limit Hold'em, where the
continuous bet-sizing dimension is expected to deepen the trap and
stress the classification ceiling.
