\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

Imagine sitting down at an 8-player poker table. You do not know
anything about the other seven players, but as hands play out you
start forming a picture: this one bets only when she has a strong
hand, that one bluffs almost every street, the third folds to any
pressure. The mental model you are building is a form of
\textit{reputation}: a belief, updated from observation, about how
each opponent is likely to play in the future. Should you bet
against the player with the cooperative reputation? Or fold to
her, because her bet probably means she really has the hand? More
generally: in a system that rewards reading the room, does
behaving honestly make you safer---or does it make you predictable
enough to exploit?

The same question applies far outside poker. Reputation systems
that infer trustworthiness from observed behavior are now standard
infrastructure on eBay, on lending platforms, in social media
moderation, and in high-frequency trading. The systems are
explicitly designed to reward cooperative behavior, but a body of
empirical work on the \textit{transparency paradox}
\citep{resnick2002trust,bolton2004effective,glosten1985bid}
documents the opposite effect in several real-world settings:
sellers with perfect feedback scores sometimes earn lower margins,
predictable market-makers face adverse selection, and content
moderation systems handle extreme accounts well but stumble in
the moderate middle. These results are observational, drawn from
data where the experimenter cannot manipulate the agents
themselves. We complement that literature with a controlled
generative test: we build a poker simulation in which every agent
maintains a probability distribution over the \textit{type} of
every other agent, we vary the agents' capability across four
architectures while keeping the reputation system fixed, and we
measure whether the most-trusted agents really do come out ahead.

Our research question is whether, in our zero-sum testbed,
observation-based trust inference rewards exploitation over
cooperation, and whether the size of this effect depends on agent
architecture.

The contributions of the paper are three.
\textit{First}, we report a controlled trust--profit ladder
across four agent architectures: $\rtp$ falls from $-0.752$ under
frozen rule-based agents to $-0.094$ under language-model agents
augmented with reasoning scaffolds (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}). The
phase ladder is robust across all five seeds in rank order but
the absolute size of the Phase 3.1 effect carries wide uncertainty
($n=5$, 150 hands/seed). \textit{Second}, a sub-experiment
(Phase~2$^{*}$) in which the bounded hill-climber is given the
full unit hypercube and $11\times$ more parameter drift fails to
close the trap and yields divergent rather than convergent
trajectories in 36-dimensional parameter space---preliminary
evidence that the trap is not a bound-box artifact in this
simulation. \textit{Third}, we provide a controlled testbed in which
inter-archetype distance is an explicit design variable; in the
present paper we set that distance to zero for three archetypes as
a sanity check, which establishes the trivial identifiability floor
at $\log_2(3)$ bits. How rapidly classification accuracy degrades
as the distance shrinks toward zero in more realistic type spaces
is a methodological direction the present testbed is designed to
support but does not itself answer.

\section{Background}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Trust, reputation, and the transparency paradox}
The theoretical foundation for studying trust in repeated
interaction is the Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma (IPD) and its
evolutionary generalizations. Axelrod's tournaments
\citep{axelrod1984evolution} established that Tit-for-Tat is robust
in \textit{pairwise} interactions, and Nowak's five rules
\citep{nowak2006five} formalize the conditions under which
cooperative strategies invade defector populations. Both results
rest on two assumptions that fail in many real-world settings:
interactions are pairwise, and the cost of defection is
concentrated on the defector. When either assumption breaks---as
in an 8-player game where an aggressor's cost is spread across
seven opponents---the canonical findings need not transfer, a
point our Tit-for-Tat analogue (\arch{Mirror}, sixth of eight in
economic ordering) makes concrete later in the paper.

A parallel empirical literature in reputation economics documents
the surprising flip side: when an agent's cooperative behavior
becomes \textit{too} legible, it can be exploited. Resnick and
Zeckhauser \citep{resnick2002trust} observe that eBay sellers
with perfect feedback scores sometimes earn lower margins than
sellers with slightly imperfect records, because buyers correctly
infer that perfect-record sellers will never risk their
reputation. Bolton, Katok, and Ockenfels \citep{bolton2004effective}
reproduce the pattern in controlled laboratory experiments; a
related mechanism in financial market microstructure---adverse
selection against predictable market-makers
\citep{glosten1985bid}---produces the same general result through
information asymmetry rather than reputation per se. The empirical
case is consistent: \textit{predictable cooperation is
exploitable}. What the literature lacks is a controlled generative
test in which agent design can be manipulated while the reputation
system itself is held fixed---which is what we build.

\subsection{Type inference from observed play}
The complementary computational question---how an opponent's type
can be inferred from observed behavior---has been developed most
deeply in poker. Billings et al.\ \citep{billings1998opponent}
introduced Bayesian opponent modeling in the Loki and Poki
systems; Southey et al.\ \citep{southey2005bayes} formalized the
machinery as Bayesian type inference; Ganzfried and Sandholm
\citep{ganzfried2015safe} extended it to the question of
\textit{safe} exploitation. In parallel, Nash-equilibrium
computation produced the solution of heads-up limit Hold'em
\citep{bowling2015heads} and the superhuman performance of
Libratus and Pluribus
\citep{brown2018superhuman,brown2019superhuman} in heads-up and
6-player no-limit Hold'em. All of this work targets optimal or
safely-exploitative play against one opponent or in zero-sum
settings; the multi-agent reputation question is largely outside
its scope.

A newer line of work asks whether large language models behave as
strategic opponent modelers when deployed as agents
\citep{park2023generative,liu2024agentbench}. The open question is
whether an LLM given a description of a strategy invents the
strategy or merely imitates its textual form. Our contrast between
Phase 3 (LLM with personality spec) and Phase 3.1 (LLM with
chain-of-thought, persistent per-opponent memory, and self-updated
strategy notes) is designed to test exactly this distinction:
when the same LLM is given the same personality spec but
additionally allowed to reason about what other players are
doing, does its behavior change in ways the spec alone did not
predict?

\subsection{Poker as a controlled testbed}
Poker is a canonical incomplete-information domain
\citep{vonneumann1944theory,harsanyi1967games} combining four
properties that isolate trust dynamics from confounders:
stochastic hidden information (hole cards), sequential decisions
with public state (betting rounds), a rich action space that
supports both cooperative and exploitative play, and explicit
chip-denominated payoffs that make economic performance directly
measurable. Limit Hold'em additionally constrains bet sizes to a
four-bet cap per street, discretizing the action distribution and
keeping the Bayesian posterior tractable.

\section{Methodology}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Game environment}
The game environment is fixed across every phase of the study. We
use 8-player fixed-limit Texas Hold'em with small blind 1, big
blind 2, small bet 2 (preflop\footnote{The four betting rounds in
Texas Hold'em, in order, are \textit{preflop} (after each player
receives two private ``hole'' cards but before any community
cards), \textit{flop} (after three community cards are dealt
simultaneously), \textit{turn} (after a fourth community card),
and \textit{river} (after a fifth and final community card).} and
flop), big bet 4 (turn and river), and a four-bet
cap\footnote{Fixed-limit Texas Hold'em permits at most four raises
per betting round; further raises are not allowed.} per street.
Each seat starts with a 200-chip stack and is granted a rebuy
whenever it busts. Showdowns\footnote{A \textit{showdown} is the
end-of-hand reveal in which all still-active players turn over
their hole cards to determine the winner; pots that end with all
opponents folding to a single player do not reach showdown.} are
awarded via side pots\footnote{When a player goes all-in for fewer
chips than the largest contributor, the engine creates a
\textit{side pot} for the excess so that the short-stacked player
can only win up to the amount they contributed; deeper-stacked
players contest the remainder among themselves.} constructed at
each per-seat contribution level, with ties split and any remainder
going to the earliest clockwise seat from the dealer.

\subsection{The eight agent archetypes}
We construct eight strategically distinct archetypes spanning the
axes that game theory and behavioral economics identify as
load-bearing: cooperative versus exploitative, static versus
adaptive, per-action versus per-opponent in adaptation style. Each
agent's policy is a per-round table of action probabilities
conditioned on hand-strength bucket; the parameters of the static
archetypes are frozen across runs. Each archetype carries an
\textit{honesty score} $h \in [0,1]$ defined as
$P(\text{bet}\mid\text{strong})\cdot(1-P(\text{bluff}\mid\text{weak}))$,
where a \textit{bluff}\footnote{A \textit{bluff} is a bet made with a weak
hand intended to make opponents fold stronger hands. Its inverse, a
\textit{value bet}, is a bet with a strong hand intended to be called by
weaker hands. \textit{Fold equity} is the value an aggressor captures when
opponents fold without reaching showdown.} is a bet with a weak hand and a
value bet is its inverse with a strong hand; this scalar is what the trust
score weights
(Eq.~\ref{eq:trust}).

\subsection{Categorical posterior over types}
Every agent maintains a length-8 categorical distribution over the
archetype of every other seat. After agent $i$ observes seat $j$
take action $a$ in betting round $r$, the distribution is updated as
\begin{equation}
  p_{i\to j}(t)\,\propto\,
    \bigl[(1-\varepsilon)\,L(a\mid t,r)
          + \varepsilon/|\mathcal{T}|\bigr]\,
    p_{i\to j}^{\text{prior}}(t),
  \label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
where $L(a\mid t, r)$ is a precomputed likelihood that a type-$t$
agent emits action $a$ in round $r$, $\mathcal{T}$ is the set of
eight archetypes, and $\varepsilon = 0.05$ is a noise floor
preventing any type from being fully eliminated. We characterize
Eq.~\ref{eq:posterior} as Bayes-flavoured rather than strictly
Bayesian: the noise floor is embedded in the likelihood
(equivalent to a uniform contamination model), the likelihood
table is computed from each archetype's analytic policy and is
therefore correctly specified by construction, and the
between-hand shrinkage in Eq.~\ref{eq:decay} is a forgetting
heuristic rather than a posterior update. Between hands,
posteriors are shrunk toward the uniform prior:
\begin{equation}
  p_{i\to j} \;\leftarrow\;
    \lambda\,p_{i\to j} + (1-\lambda)\,|\mathcal{T}|^{-1},
  \qquad \lambda = 0.95,
  \label{eq:decay}
\end{equation}
giving an effective memory of approximately $1/(1-\lambda) = 20$
hands. The trust score, our primary dependent variable, is
\begin{equation}
  \text{trust}_{i\to j} \;=\;
    \sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\, p_{i\to j}(t)\, h(t),
  \label{eq:trust}
\end{equation}
where $h(t) = P(\text{bet}\mid\text{strong})\cdot
(1-P(\text{bluff}\mid\text{weak}))$ is the archetype's honesty
score. This functional form is one of several plausible
definitions (alternatives include the mean of the two
probabilities or an entropy-based measure); we report results
under the product form throughout and note in the limitations
that the trust--profit correlation is mechanically anchored on
this choice. \textit{Three archetypes (\arch{Sentinel},
\arch{Mirror} in its default state, \arch{Judge} in its
cooperative state) are intentionally stipulated with identical
mean parameters to provide a controlled demonstration of
identifiability under indistinguishable types}; this is a design
choice, not a finding.

\subsection{Four agent architectures}
The four phases differ only in the implementation of each agent's
decision policy. \textbf{Phase 1} (frozen rule-based): each
archetype is a fixed per-round parameter table. \textbf{Phase 2}
(bounded online optimization): each agent runs a per-cycle hill-
climber that, every 200 hands, perturbs one (round, metric) slot by
$\delta = 0.03$, plays a 200-hand trial, and accepts the
perturbation if windowed profit improves; perturbations are
confined to an archetype-shaped bound box. \textbf{Phase 3} (LLM
personality role-players): each agent's decision is an API call to
Anthropic's Claude Haiku 4.5 with the archetype's personality spec
as the system prompt. \textbf{Phase 3.1} (LLM with reasoning
scaffolding): the Phase 3 setup augmented with chain-of-thought
prompting capped at two sentences, persistent per-opponent memory
refreshed every 10 hands, and self-updated strategy notes refreshed
every 25 hands. A sub-experiment \textbf{Phase 2$^{*}$} removes the
hill-climber's bound boxes entirely and raises the step size to
$\delta = 0.15$ with a 50-hand evaluation window.

\subsection{Experimental design}
All four phases use the same five random seeds (42, 137, 256, 512,
1024) and the same eight archetype assignments. \textit{Hand counts
differ across phases by an order of magnitude:} Phases 1, 2, and
2$^{*}$ each play 10\,000 hands per seed; Phase 3 plays 500 hands
per seed (43\,943 total LLM calls, \$33.10); Phase 3.1 plays 150
hands per seed (11\,953 calls, ${\sim}\$17$). This asymmetry is
imposed by API cost and is a known confound in cross-phase
comparisons of per-seed $\rtp$: even under an identical underlying
trust--profit relationship, the per-seed correlation in Phase 3.1
would be measured on a noisier $n$ and would be attenuated in
magnitude. The reported $\Delta\rtp$ between phases therefore mixes
a capability effect with a statistical-power effect, an issue we
address explicitly in the Discussion (\S\,Limitations) and which a
hand-count-matched replication would resolve.

\textit{Sampling temperature.} The canonical Phase 3 and Phase 3.1
datasets were generated under the Anthropic SDK's default
temperature of 1.0; the codebase now pins temperature to 0.0 for
deterministic re-runs. Headline numbers throughout this paper are
from the temperature-1.0 runs. The full disclosure of this
methodological caveat is in the Limitations.

\subsection{Metrics}
The primary metric is the per-seed Pearson correlation $\rtp$
between mean trust score (final hand) and final stack across the
eight archetypes ($n = 8$ paired observations per seed). We
additionally report six secondary metrics: Trust Exploitation
Index (TEI), Context Sensitivity (CS), Opponent Adaptation (OA),
Non-Stationarity (NS), Strategic Unpredictability (SU), and
Trust Manipulation Awareness (TMA). TMA is the correlation between
an agent's rolling trust delta and its rolling aggression delta;
positive values capture the pattern of \textit{building} trust
through cooperative early play and subsequently \textit{exploiting}
that trust with higher aggression, an interaction we refer to as
``trust farming'' in the captions and discussion. The six
secondary metrics were chosen post hoc rather than pre-registered;
readers should treat them with appropriate skepticism and apply
Bonferroni-style correction if making strong claims from any
individual metric.
Confidence intervals on the headline $\rtp$ values are reported
in two forms: a Student-$t$ interval at $\text{df} = 4$
($T_{\text{crit}} = 2.776$) and a non-parametric percentile
bootstrap with 10\,000 resamples. The bootstrap on $n = 5$ seeds
draws from at most $\binom{2n-1}{n} = 126$ distinct combinations,
so the 10\,000 resamples densely cover the resampling space but do
not buy additional statistical power beyond the five underlying
observations.

\subsection{The four-tier ladder}
Across the four canonical agent architectures, the Pearson
correlation between trust and stack follows the four-tier ladder
of Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}, tabulated in
Table~\ref{tab:ladder}. Phase 1 produces $\rtp = -0.752 \pm 0.073$;
Phase 2 softens to $-0.637 \pm 0.125$; Phase 2$^{*}$ to $-0.609 \pm
0.221$; Phase 3 to $-0.510 \pm 0.268$; Phase 3.1 to $-0.094 \pm
0.301$. The 95\% Student-$t$ interval on the Phase 3.1 mean is
$[-0.512, +0.323]$; the bootstrap interval is $[-0.324, +0.203]$.
Both intervals contain zero \textit{and} a moderate negative
correlation in the same range as the bounded Phase 2 effect; the
present data are therefore consistent with substantial attenuation
of the trust--profit correlation under reasoning scaffolds but do
not, at $n=5$ seeds, establish that the correlation is zero. We
report the rank ordering of the four phases (P1 $\to$ P2 $\to$
P3 $\to$ P3.1 by absolute size of $\rtp$) as the most robust
finding; the absolute size of each step carries the
corresponding interval-level uncertainty.

\section{Discussion}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{The mechanism behind the trap}
The trust trap visible in Phase 1 admits a clean mechanistic
explanation that the Phase 3.1 results then refine. Trust, as we
measure it, is the expected honesty of an opponent under the
posterior; a high trust score signals to opponents that the agent
is most likely an archetype that only bets with strong hands. For
\arch{Wall} (honesty 0.96) and \arch{Sentinel} (honesty 0.92),
this belief is \textit{correct}, and that is precisely why it is
costly: when opponents know an agent only bets with strong hands,
they fold against its bets (denying value from those strong
hands) and bet aggressively against its checks (exploiting the
information that a check signals weakness). The agent's honesty
becomes a signal that opponents read and trade against. A low
trust score, conversely, creates strategic ambiguity, and
\arch{Firestorm} uses that ambiguity to dominate the table. The
phenomenon parallels what economists studying eBay-style
reputation systems have called the \textit{transparency paradox}
\citep{resnick2002trust}: predictable cooperation is exploitable.

The mechanism by which \arch{Firestorm} converts ambiguity into
chips deserves comment because it differs structurally from the
canonical Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma result. In pairwise IPD,
Always-Defect is outperformed by Tit-for-Tat because a defector
faces concentrated retaliation. In our 8-player setting,
\arch{Firestorm}'s aggression cost is diffused across seven
simultaneous opponents, so the same strategy that would lose
heads-up wins at the full table. Our Tit-for-Tat analogue
(\arch{Mirror}) finishes sixth of eight in economic ordering---
suggestive but not definitive evidence that the IPD result fails
to transfer to multi-agent settings with richer action spaces. We
flag this as a direction for follow-up rather than a conclusion,
since a single hand-crafted poker analogue is not a clean test of
the IPD literature.

\subsection{What numerical optimization can and cannot do}
The next three phases of the experiment narrow the space of
possible explanations for why the trap is hard to escape. We
begin with a structural caveat: by construction, three of our
eight archetypes (\arch{Sentinel}, \arch{Mirror}'s default,
\arch{Judge}'s cooperative state) share byte-identical mean
parameters, and the Bayesian posterior cannot distinguish them
regardless of algorithmic sophistication. Classification accuracy
in that sub-space is bounded below by the entropy of a uniform
distribution over the three members. This is a definitional
point about \textit{our} archetype space, not an information-
theoretic discovery about reputation systems in general; the
empirical question of how rapidly classification accuracy degrades
as inter-archetype distance shrinks toward zero would require
experimentally varying that distance and is outside the scope of
the present paper. Real-world monitoring systems---fraud
detection, content moderation, credit scoring---face an analogous
failure mode for moderately-behaved actors with overlapping
profiles, but the analogy is suggestive rather than implied by
our setup.

Within that caveat, the Phase 2 and Phase 2$^{*}$ results argue
against two specific hypotheses about the trap's origin. Phase 2
(bounded hill-climbing) softens $\rtp$ by $+0.116$ while
$\mathrm{OA}$ stays pinned at $0.0003$: aggregate-reward
optimization can move agent parameters but cannot, by
construction, produce per-opponent strategy because the
hill-climber has no per-opponent parameter slot. Phase 2$^{*}$
(unbounded hill-climbing with $11\times$ greater parameter drift)
softens $\rtp$ by an additional $+0.028$ and produces a population
that diverges rather than converges in 36-dimensional parameter
space---evidence against the hypothesis that the archetype-
shaped bound boxes are the binding constraint, though the
sub-experiment varies three optimizer parameters at once
(bounds removed, step size raised $5\times$, evaluation window
shrunk $4\times$) and therefore does not cleanly isolate the
parameter-space hypothesis. The non-convergence has three
plausible structural causes that we expect would survive any
specific optimizer choice: the trust posterior is non-stationary
inside each agent's optimization loop (with $\lambda = 0.95$,
opponents take ${\sim}70$ hands to update beliefs after a
strategy change but the evaluation window is 50 hands), all eight
agents climb simultaneously so each trial perturbation is
evaluated against a moving joint behavior, and axis-aligned
coordinate descent in 36 dimensions cannot discover joint optima
even with larger steps. A CMA-ES or REINFORCE optimizer with
${\sim}50\,000$ cycles would search more efficiently, but the
first two causes would survive any optimization substrate that
respects the same observation contract.

Phase 3 (LLM personality role-players) softens $\rtp$ by a
further $+0.127$, but the diagnostic metrics suggest the
mechanism is not categorically different from Phase 2's:
behavioral-dimension targets fail in three of four directions,
with the LLMs collapsing onto a more stereotyped interpretation
of each archetype than the explicitly stochastic rule-based
versions. The personality specs tell the agents \textit{what} to
do but not \textit{how} to reason; absent reasoning support the
LLMs role-play the spec without spontaneously inventing
opponent-conditional, time-varying, or unpredictable strategy.
The Phase 3 softening may therefore be produced by output
stochasticity at sampling temperature 1.0 rather than by a
structural change in agent behavior.

\subsection{What reasoning adds, and what we can and cannot claim
outside poker}
The Phase 3.1 result is consistent with an interpretation in
which agent reasoning about the reputation system itself
attenuates the trust--profit correlation. The chain-of-thought
prompt allows a one-step lookahead; the per-opponent memory
gives that reasoning concrete data to condition on; the
adaptive strategy notes let the agent update its prior on what
is working against which opponent. We claim that all three
scaffolds are required, but this claim rests on an ablation
reported in the supplementary validation suite rather than a
dedicated main-text figure---the ablation deserves promotion in
a future revision. The \arch{Wall} hand reproduced in Box~2
illustrates the mechanism concretely in a single instance:
\arch{Wall} reads \arch{Firestorm}'s river check as a tell,
recognizes that K-Q high beats \arch{Firestorm}'s bluffing
range, and bets for value---a sequence that is impossible for
the Phase 1 \arch{Wall} and unreliable for the Phase 3
\arch{Wall}. A distributional analysis across all Phase 3.1
hands, rather than a single illustrative case, is the natural
follow-up.

These mechanisms suggest hypotheses for real-world reputation
systems, but we emphasize the limit of the analogy: our testbed
is a zero-sum game and the proposed exploitation mechanism
(predictable cooperation extracted via fold equity) does not
directly map to positive-sum marketplaces, where the buyer's
payoff is from the good rather than from out-thinking the seller.
With that caveat, three implications follow as conjectures for
cross-domain follow-up. \textit{Behavioral reputation alone may
be insufficient} without external enforcement, because
observation-based trust can create perverse incentives that
reward exploitative strategies. \textit{Classification systems
may have blind spots} for moderately-behaved actors with
overlapping profiles, mirroring our identifiability sub-space.
And \textit{transparency itself may be costly} when it makes
cooperative strategies too easy to model and exploit. Each of
these would require direct validation in a positive-sum domain
to claim more than analogical inference.

\subsection{Limitations}
Three clusters of limitations remain after the revisions above.

\textit{Statistical power gaps.} The headline correlations are
computed across $n = 5$ random seeds; the Student-$t$ critical
value at $\text{df} = 4$ is 2.776, which requires $|\rtp| >
0.878$ for two-sided significance against zero. The headline
Phase 3.1 result has a 95\% bootstrap interval $[-0.32, +0.20]$
that contains both a fully neutralized correlation and a
moderate residual negative correlation, so we cannot
statistically separate ``trap broken'' from ``trap attenuated''
at this sample size. Hand counts also differ across phases by an
order of magnitude (10\,000 hands/seed in Phases 1--2$^{*}$, 500
in Phase 3, 150 in Phase 3.1), which mixes a capability effect
with a statistical-power effect in the cross-phase ladder. The
six secondary metrics (TEI, CS, OA, NS, SU, TMA) were chosen
post hoc and are unadjusted for multiple comparisons. The single
highest-leverage follow-up addressing all of these is an $n = 20$
Phase 3.1 replication at the 500-hand horizon, estimated at
${\sim}\$60$ in API spend.

\textit{Reproducibility and replication gaps.} The canonical
Phase 3 and 3.1 datasets were generated under the Anthropic SDK's
default sampling temperature of 1.0, while the released codebase
now pins temperature to 0.0; the headline numbers are therefore
not exactly reproducible from the current code without manual
reversion. Phase 3 and 3.1 also use a single model family
(Anthropic Haiku 4.5); a multi-model replication (Gemini Flash,
GPT-4o-mini, or a comparable alternative) is needed to
demonstrate that the attenuation is not model-specific. Finally,
the Phase 3.1 three-scaffold deployment (chain-of-thought,
memory, adaptive notes) is currently joint; a clean main-text
ablation isolating each component's contribution is the second-
most-important missing experiment.

\textit{Design-choice gaps.} Several of our methodological
choices are load-bearing in ways that future work should
disambiguate. The identifiability ``floor'' is a stipulated
property of our archetype space (three archetypes share mean
parameters), not an emergent finding; the honesty score
$h(t) = P(\text{bet}\mid\text{strong}) \cdot
(1 - P(\text{bluff}\mid\text{weak}))$ is one of several
plausible functional forms and the trust--profit correlation is
mechanically anchored on this choice; the rebuy economics
underweight deep chip losses (\arch{Wall}'s 9.4 mean rebuys per
seed in Phase 1 represent ${\sim}1\,880$ chips of true loss not
visible in the final-stack metric); the Phase 2$^{*}$ sub-
experiment varies three optimizer parameters at once; and Limit
Hold'em is zero-sum among players, while eBay marketplaces,
social networks, and credit systems are positive-sum over trades,
so direct cross-domain inference is rhetorical rather than
warranted.

% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =============================================================================

We have presented a controlled simulation study of trust dynamics
in 8-player Limit Texas Hold'em. Across four agent architectures,
the Pearson correlation between agent trust score and final stack
falls along the ladder $-0.752 \to -0.637 \to -0.510 \to -0.094$,
with each step accompanied by a confidence interval whose width
exceeds the size of any single inter-phase $\Delta\rtp$. We
characterize the rank ordering of the ladder as the robust finding
and the absolute size of each step as a preliminary estimate
requiring replication. An unbounded hill-climbing sub-experiment
with $11\times$ greater parameter drift produces $\Delta\rtp =
+0.028$ from bounded Phase 2 and yields a population that diverges
rather than converges in 36-dimensional parameter space---
preliminary evidence against the hypothesis that the trap is a
bound-box artifact, though Phase 2$^{*}$ varies three optimizer
parameters at once and does not cleanly isolate the bounds.

Three follow-up experiments are the most important next steps.
\textit{First}, an $n = 20$ Phase 3.1 replication at 500 hands per
seed (matching Phase 3) is the highest-leverage single experiment:
it would tighten the confidence interval on the Phase 3.1 result by
roughly a factor of two and distinguish ``trap attenuated'' from
``trap broken''; estimated cost ${\sim}\$60$ in API spend.
\textit{Second}, a clean ablation of the three Phase 3.1 scaffolds
(chain-of-thought, per-opponent memory, adaptive notes) is required
to support the claim that all three are necessary.
\textit{Third}, a temperature-pinned re-run of Phase 3 and Phase
3.1 at $T = 0.0$ would resolve the open reproducibility caveat.
Beyond these three, a multi-LLM replication, a no-limit Hold'em
extension, and an experimental varying of inter-archetype distance
would each strengthen the present findings.
