\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

Trust is a foundational element of repeated strategic interaction. In
settings where intentions cannot be directly verified---online
marketplaces, anonymous networks, international diplomacy---
trustworthiness must be inferred from observed behavior over many
interactions. Modern reputation mechanisms formalize this inference:
a buyer rates a seller, a lender evaluates a borrower's credit
history, a social network ranks contributors by past engagement. The
mechanism now mediates the default operating dynamic of most digital
commerce.

A long-standing empirical question is whether these
observation-based systems reward the behavior they are designed to
detect. Prior work documents a \textit{transparency paradox} in
several real-world domains \citep{resnick2002trust,
bolton2004effective}: eBay sellers with perfect feedback scores
sometimes earn lower margins than sellers with slightly imperfect
records, predictable market-makers face adverse selection, and
content moderation systems struggle with the moderate middle of the
behavioral distribution. These results are observational, drawn
from datasets in which agent design is not under the experimenter's
control. We complement that literature with a controlled
generative test in which agent capability is the manipulated
variable and the reputation system itself is held fixed.

We construct an 8-player Limit Texas Hold'em simulation in which
every agent maintains a categorical posterior over the type of
every other agent, deploy four agent architectures against the
same fixed environment, and measure the correlation between trust
and economic performance.

Our research question is whether, in our zero-sum testbed,
observation-based trust inference rewards exploitation over
cooperation, and whether the size of this effect depends on agent
architecture.

The contributions of the paper are three.
\textit{First}, we report a controlled trust--profit ladder
across four agent architectures: $\rtp$ falls from $-0.752$ under
frozen rule-based agents to $-0.094$ under language-model agents
augmented with reasoning scaffolds (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}). The
phase ladder is robust across all five seeds in rank order but
the absolute size of the Phase 3.1 effect carries wide uncertainty
($n=5$, 150 hands/seed). \textit{Second}, a sub-experiment
(Phase~2$^{*}$) in which the bounded hill-climber is given the
full unit hypercube and $11\times$ more parameter drift fails to
close the trap and yields divergent rather than convergent
trajectories in 36-dimensional parameter space---preliminary
evidence that the trap is not a bound-box artifact in this
simulation. \textit{Third}, we provide a controlled testbed in which
inter-archetype distance is an explicit design variable; in the
present paper we set that distance to zero for three archetypes as
a sanity check, which establishes the trivial identifiability floor
at $\log_2(3)$ bits. How rapidly classification accuracy degrades
as the distance shrinks toward zero in more realistic type spaces
is a methodological direction the present testbed is designed to
support but does not itself answer.

\section{Background}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Cooperation in repeated games}
Research on cooperation in repeated games has been dominated by the
Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma (IPD) and its evolutionary
generalizations. Axelrod's tournaments \citep{axelrod1984evolution} established that
Tit-for-Tat is robust in pairwise interactions, and Nowak's five
rules \citep{nowak2006five} formalize the conditions under which cooperative
strategies invade defector populations. These findings rest on
pairwise interaction and on payoff structures that concentrate
defection costs on the defector. Neither property is preserved in
multi-agent settings with richer action spaces---a point we return
to in the discussion when we report that our Tit-for-Tat analogue
(\arch{Mirror}) finishes sixth of eight in economic ordering.

\subsection{Bayesian opponent modeling and exploitation}
Bayesian opponent modeling in poker has a long history, beginning
with Billings et al.\ on the Loki and Poki systems
\citep{billings1998opponent}, formalized as Bayesian type inference
by Southey et al.\ \citep{southey2005bayes}, and extended to the
question of \textit{safe} opponent exploitation by Ganzfried and
Sandholm \citep{ganzfried2015safe}. Parallel work on Nash-
equilibrium computation produced the solution of heads-up limit
Hold'em \citep{bowling2015heads} and the superhuman performance of
Libratus and Pluribus in no-limit Hold'em
\citep{brown2018superhuman,brown2019superhuman}. This body of work
is concerned with optimal or safely-exploitative play against a
single opponent or in heads-up zero-sum settings. Our work uses
poker as a controlled testbed for how the underlying type-inference
machinery interacts with multi-agent reputation dynamics, rather
than as a benchmark to solve.

\subsection{Reputation in economics}
Reputation in economics has been studied empirically by Resnick and
Zeckhauser \citep{resnick2002trust} and Bolton, Katok, and Ockenfels
\citep{bolton2004effective}, who document what is sometimes called
the \textit{transparency paradox}: agents with perfect, easily-
readable reputations earn lower margins because buyers correctly
infer that they will never risk those reputations. A parallel
mechanism in financial market microstructure---adverse selection
against predictable market-makers \citep{glosten1985bid}---produces
the same general pattern, though through information asymmetry
rather than reputation per se. Our simulation provides a controlled
generative analogue of these empirical findings.

\subsection{LLM agents in strategic settings}
A more recent line of work asks whether large language models
behave strategically when deployed as agents in adversarial or
benchmark settings \citep{park2023generative,liu2024agentbench}.
A core open question is whether LLMs invent strategy when given a
description of strategy or merely imitate the textual form of that
description. Our contrast between Phase 3 (LLM with personality
spec only) and Phase 3.1 (LLM with reasoning scaffolding) is
designed to test this distinction directly.

\subsection{Poker as a testbed}
Poker is a canonical incomplete-information domain \citep{vonneumann1944theory,harsanyi1967games}
combining four properties that isolate trust dynamics from
confounders: stochastic hidden information, sequential decisions
with public state, a rich action space, and explicit chip-
denominated payoffs. Limit Hold'em constrains the bet sizes to a
four-bet cap per street, discretizing the action distribution and
keeping the Bayesian posterior tractable.

\section{Methodology}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Game environment}
The game environment is fixed across every phase of the study. We
use 8-player fixed-limit Texas Hold'em with small blind 1, big
blind 2, small bet 2 (preflop and flop), big bet 4 (turn and
river), and a four-bet cap per street. Each seat starts with a 200-
chip stack and is granted a rebuy whenever it busts. Showdowns are
awarded via side pots constructed at each per-seat contribution
level, with ties split and any remainder going to the earliest
clockwise seat from the dealer.

\subsection{The eight agent archetypes}
We construct eight strategically distinct archetypes spanning the
axes that game theory and behavioral economics identify as
load-bearing: cooperative versus exploitative, static versus
adaptive, per-action versus per-opponent in adaptation style. Each
agent's policy is a per-round table of action probabilities
conditioned on hand-strength bucket; the parameters of the static
archetypes are frozen across runs. Each archetype carries an
\textit{honesty score} $h \in [0,1]$ defined as
$P(\text{bet}\mid\text{strong})\cdot(1-P(\text{bluff}\mid\text{weak}))$;
this scalar is what the trust score weights
(Eq.~\ref{eq:trust}).

\subsection{Categorical posterior over types}
Every agent maintains a length-8 categorical distribution over the
archetype of every other seat. After agent $i$ observes seat $j$
take action $a$ in betting round $r$, the distribution is updated as
\begin{equation}
  p_{i\to j}(t)\,\propto\,
    \bigl[(1-\varepsilon)\,L(a\mid t,r)
          + \varepsilon/|\mathcal{T}|\bigr]\,
    p_{i\to j}^{\text{prior}}(t),
  \label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
where $L(a\mid t, r)$ is a precomputed likelihood that a type-$t$
agent emits action $a$ in round $r$, $\mathcal{T}$ is the set of
eight archetypes, and $\varepsilon = 0.05$ is a noise floor
preventing any type from being fully eliminated. We characterize
Eq.~\ref{eq:posterior} as Bayes-flavoured rather than strictly
Bayesian: the noise floor is embedded in the likelihood
(equivalent to a uniform contamination model), the likelihood
table is computed from each archetype's analytic policy and is
therefore correctly specified by construction, and the
between-hand shrinkage in Eq.~\ref{eq:decay} is a forgetting
heuristic rather than a posterior update. Between hands,
posteriors are shrunk toward the uniform prior:
\begin{equation}
  p_{i\to j} \;\leftarrow\;
    \lambda\,p_{i\to j} + (1-\lambda)\,|\mathcal{T}|^{-1},
  \qquad \lambda = 0.95,
  \label{eq:decay}
\end{equation}
giving an effective memory of approximately $1/(1-\lambda) = 20$
hands. The trust score, our primary dependent variable, is
\begin{equation}
  \text{trust}_{i\to j} \;=\;
    \sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\, p_{i\to j}(t)\, h(t),
  \label{eq:trust}
\end{equation}
where $h(t) = P(\text{bet}\mid\text{strong})\cdot
(1-P(\text{bluff}\mid\text{weak}))$ is the archetype's honesty
score. This functional form is one of several plausible
definitions (alternatives include the mean of the two
probabilities or an entropy-based measure); we report results
under the product form throughout and note in the limitations
that the trust--profit correlation is mechanically anchored on
this choice. \textit{Three archetypes (\arch{Sentinel},
\arch{Mirror} in its default state, \arch{Judge} in its
cooperative state) are intentionally stipulated with identical
mean parameters to provide a controlled demonstration of
identifiability under indistinguishable types}; this is a design
choice, not a finding.

\subsection{Four agent architectures}
The four phases differ only in the implementation of each agent's
decision policy. \textbf{Phase 1} (frozen rule-based): each
archetype is a fixed per-round parameter table. \textbf{Phase 2}
(bounded online optimization): each agent runs a per-cycle hill-
climber that, every 200 hands, perturbs one (round, metric) slot by
$\delta = 0.03$, plays a 200-hand trial, and accepts the
perturbation if windowed profit improves; perturbations are
confined to an archetype-shaped bound box. \textbf{Phase 3} (LLM
personality role-players): each agent's decision is an API call to
Anthropic's Claude Haiku 4.5 with the archetype's personality spec
as the system prompt. \textbf{Phase 3.1} (LLM with reasoning
scaffolding): the Phase 3 setup augmented with chain-of-thought
prompting capped at two sentences, persistent per-opponent memory
refreshed every 10 hands, and self-updated strategy notes refreshed
every 25 hands. A sub-experiment \textbf{Phase 2$^{*}$} removes the
hill-climber's bound boxes entirely and raises the step size to
$\delta = 0.15$ with a 50-hand evaluation window.

\subsection{Experimental design}
All four phases use the same five random seeds (42, 137, 256, 512,
1024) and the same eight archetype assignments. \textit{Hand counts
differ across phases by an order of magnitude:} Phases 1, 2, and
2$^{*}$ each play 10\,000 hands per seed; Phase 3 plays 500 hands
per seed (43\,943 total LLM calls, \$33.10); Phase 3.1 plays 150
hands per seed (11\,953 calls, ${\sim}\$17$). This asymmetry is
imposed by API cost and is a known confound in cross-phase
comparisons of per-seed $\rtp$: even under an identical underlying
trust--profit relationship, the per-seed correlation in Phase 3.1
would be measured on a noisier $n$ and would be attenuated in
magnitude. The reported $\Delta\rtp$ between phases therefore mixes
a capability effect with a statistical-power effect, an issue we
address explicitly in the Discussion (\S\,Limitations) and which a
hand-count-matched replication would resolve.

\textit{Sampling temperature.} The canonical Phase 3 and Phase 3.1
datasets were generated under the Anthropic SDK's default
temperature of 1.0; the codebase now pins temperature to 0.0 for
deterministic re-runs. Headline numbers throughout this paper are
from the temperature-1.0 runs. The full disclosure of this
methodological caveat is in the Limitations.

\subsection{Metrics}
The primary metric is the per-seed Pearson correlation $\rtp$
between mean trust score (final hand) and final stack across the
eight archetypes ($n = 8$ paired observations per seed). We
additionally report six secondary metrics: Trust Exploitation
Index (TEI), Context Sensitivity (CS), Opponent Adaptation (OA),
Non-Stationarity (NS), Strategic Unpredictability (SU), and
Trust Manipulation Awareness (TMA). TMA is the correlation between
an agent's rolling trust delta and its rolling aggression delta;
positive values capture the pattern of \textit{building} trust
through cooperative early play and subsequently \textit{exploiting}
that trust with higher aggression, an interaction we refer to as
``trust farming'' in the captions and discussion. The six
secondary metrics were chosen post hoc rather than pre-registered;
readers should treat them with appropriate skepticism and apply
Bonferroni-style correction if making strong claims from any
individual metric.
Confidence intervals on the headline $\rtp$ values are reported
in two forms: a Student-$t$ interval at $\text{df} = 4$
($T_{\text{crit}} = 2.776$) and a non-parametric percentile
bootstrap with 10\,000 resamples. The bootstrap on $n = 5$ seeds
draws from at most $\binom{2n-1}{n} = 126$ distinct combinations,
so the 10\,000 resamples densely cover the resampling space but do
not buy additional statistical power beyond the five underlying
observations.

\subsection{The four-tier ladder}
Across the four canonical agent architectures, the Pearson
correlation between trust and stack follows the four-tier ladder
of Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}, tabulated in
Table~\ref{tab:ladder}. Phase 1 produces $\rtp = -0.752 \pm 0.073$;
Phase 2 softens to $-0.637 \pm 0.125$; Phase 2$^{*}$ to $-0.609 \pm
0.221$; Phase 3 to $-0.510 \pm 0.268$; Phase 3.1 to $-0.094 \pm
0.301$. The 95\% Student-$t$ interval on the Phase 3.1 mean is
$[-0.512, +0.323]$; the bootstrap interval is $[-0.324, +0.203]$.
Both intervals contain zero \textit{and} a moderate negative
correlation in the same range as the bounded Phase 2 effect; the
present data are therefore consistent with substantial attenuation
of the trust--profit correlation under reasoning scaffolds but do
not, at $n=5$ seeds, establish that the correlation is zero. We
report the rank ordering of the four phases (P1 $\to$ P2 $\to$
P3 $\to$ P3.1 by absolute size of $\rtp$) as the most robust
finding; the absolute size of each step carries the
corresponding interval-level uncertainty.

\subsection{Phase 1 numbers}
Phase 1 produces $\rtp = -0.752$ averaged across the five canonical
seeds, with per-seed values $\{-0.774, -0.608, -0.792, -0.812,
-0.776\}$. \arch{Wall} (the most-trusted archetype, honesty 0.96)
has the smallest mean stack and approximately 9.4 rebuys per seed.
\arch{Firestorm} (the least-trusted, honesty 0.38) has the largest
mean stack and zero rebuys. Of the pots \arch{Firestorm} wins,
$87.1\%$ are walkovers in which all opponents fold before showdown;
\arch{Firestorm}'s showdown win rate is only $38.5\%$, below the
uniform $1/n$ rate. The trust--stack relationship is shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:trust-vs-stack}.

\subsection{Classification accuracy and the identifiability ceiling}
The \arch{Predator} archetype, which reads its posterior to choose
exploitation targets, classifies only 3 of 7 opponents reliably
above the prespecified 0.60 confidence threshold after 1\,000
hands: \arch{Wall} ($p = 1.00$), \arch{Firestorm} ($p \approx
0.82$), and occasionally \arch{Phantom} ($p \approx 0.60$ in some
seeds). The remaining four opponents fall below the threshold on
every seed. Three of these (\arch{Sentinel}, \arch{Mirror} in its
default state, \arch{Judge} in its cooperative state) have byte-
identical mean parameters under the spec; the Bayesian posterior
over them bottoms out at $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits regardless
of observation volume.

\section{Discussion}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Why trust is costly}
The trust trap visible in Phase 1 admits a clean mechanistic
explanation. Trust, as we measure it, is the expected honesty of an
opponent under the posterior. A high trust score signals that the
agent is most likely an archetype that only bets with strong hands.
This belief is \textit{correct} for \arch{Wall} (honesty 0.96) and
\arch{Sentinel} (honesty 0.92), which is precisely why it is
costly: when opponents know an agent only bets with strong hands,
they fold against its bets (denying value from those strong hands)
and bet aggressively against its checks (exploiting the
information that a check signals weakness). The agent's honesty
becomes a signal that opponents read and trade against. A low trust
score creates strategic ambiguity that \arch{Firestorm} uses to
dominate the table. The phenomenon parallels what economists
studying eBay-style reputation systems have called the
\textit{transparency paradox} \citep{resnick2002trust}.

\subsection{Why aggression dominates in our multi-agent setting}
\arch{Firestorm}'s dominance is at first glance inconsistent with
canonical IPD findings, where Always-Defect is outperformed by
Tit-for-Tat. A plausible mechanism in our setting is that
\arch{Firestorm}'s aggression cost is spread across seven
simultaneous opponents rather than concentrated as in pairwise
IPD. \arch{Mirror}, our Tit-for-Tat analogue, finishes 6th of 8
in economic ordering---suggestive but not definitive evidence
about how the canonical IPD result generalizes (a single
hand-crafted poker analogue is not a clean test of the IPD
literature). We flag this as a direction for follow-up work
rather than a conclusion.

\subsection{Identifiability under indistinguishable types}
By construction, three archetypes (\arch{Sentinel}, \arch{Mirror}'s
default state, \arch{Judge}'s cooperative state) share byte-
identical mean parameters; under our likelihood model, no
observer can distinguish them regardless of algorithmic
sophistication. We use this design choice to demonstrate the
identifiability floor: when the type space contains genuinely
indistinguishable members, classification accuracy is bounded
below by the entropy of the uniform distribution over them. This
is a definitional point, not an information-theoretic discovery
about reputation systems in general. The interesting empirical
question---how rapidly classification accuracy degrades as
inter-archetype distance shrinks toward zero---requires varying
that distance experimentally and is left to future work. Real-
world monitoring systems (fraud detection, content moderation,
credit scoring) face an analogous failure mode for moderately-
behaved actors with overlapping behavioral profiles; we view that
analogy as suggestive rather than as a direct implication of our
result.

\subsection{Why numerical optimization did not escape the trap
in our experiments}
The Phase 2 and Phase 2$^{*}$ results, taken together, argue
against two specific hypotheses about the trap. The Phase 2 result
($\Delta\rtp = +0.116$ with $\mathrm{OA}$ pinned at $0.0003$)
shows that aggregate-reward optimization softens the correlation
but cannot produce per-opponent strategy by construction, since
the hill-climber has no per-opponent parameter slot. The Phase
2$^{*}$ result ($\Delta\rtp = +0.028$ with $11\times$ more parameter
drift and divergent rather than convergent cluster dynamics) is
evidence against the hypothesis that the bound boxes are the
binding constraint, though Phase 2$^{*}$ varies three things at
once (bounds removed, step size raised $5\times$, evaluation
window shrunk $4\times$) and therefore does not cleanly isolate
the parameter-space hypothesis.

The non-convergence of Phase 2$^{*}$ has three plausible
structural causes that we believe would survive any specific
hill-climber implementation. \textit{First}, the trust posterior
is non-stationary inside each agent's optimization loop: with
$\lambda = 0.95$, opponents take ${\sim}70$ hands to update their
beliefs after an agent changes strategy, but the 50-hand
evaluation window measures profit against stale beliefs.
\textit{Second}, all eight agents climb simultaneously, so each
trial perturbation is evaluated against a joint behavior that is
itself drifting. \textit{Third}, axis-aligned coordinate descent in
36 dimensions is slow even with larger steps. A CMA-ES or
REINFORCE optimizer with ${\sim}50\,000$ cycles would search more
efficiently, but reasons one and two would survive any
optimization substrate that respects the same observation contract.

\subsection{Why language models alone did not break the trap
in our experiments}
Phase 3's result admits a specific interpretation. LLMs given
personality specs appear to role-play those specs without
spontaneously inventing opponent-conditional, time-varying, or
unpredictable strategy. The personality specs tell the agents
\textit{what} to do but not \textit{how} to reason; absent
reasoning support, the LLMs in our sample collapsed onto a more
stereotyped canonical interpretation of each archetype. Three
of four behavioral-dimension metrics moved \textit{backward}
relative to Phases 1 and 2, consistent with this interpretation.
The trust--profit softening in Phase 3 may therefore be produced
by output stochasticity (temperature 1.0 in our canonical run)
rather than by a structural change in agent behavior.

\subsection{Why reasoning scaffolding may have attenuated the
trap in our experiments}
The Phase 3.1 result is consistent with an interpretation in
which agent reasoning about the reputation system itself
attenuates the trust--profit correlation. The chain-of-thought
prompt allows a one-step lookahead; the per-opponent memory gives
that reasoning concrete data to condition on; the adaptive
strategy notes let the agent update its prior on what is working
against which opponent. We claim in the manuscript that all three
scaffolds are required---this claim rests on an ablation reported
in the supplementary validation suite, and we acknowledge the
ablation deserves a dedicated main-text figure in a future
revision. The \arch{Wall} hand reproduced in Box~2 illustrates the
mechanism in a single hand: \arch{Wall} reads \arch{Firestorm}'s
river check as a tell, recognizes that K-Q high beats
\arch{Firestorm}'s bluffing range, and bets for value. This is a
single illustrative case rather than confirmatory evidence about
the rate of such reasoning moments; a distributional analysis
across all Phase 3.1 hands is left to follow-up work.

\subsection{Cross-domain implications (conjectural)}
The mechanisms identified above suggest hypotheses for real-world
reputation systems, but we emphasize that our testbed is a zero-
sum game and the proposed mechanism (predictable cooperation is
exploited via fold equity) has no direct analogue in positive-sum
marketplace settings. With that caveat, three implications
follow as conjectures for follow-up cross-domain work.
\textit{Behavioral reputation may be insufficient} without
external enforcement. \textit{Classification systems may have
blind spots} for moderately-behaved actors with overlapping
profiles. \textit{Transparency may be costly} when it makes
cooperative strategies predictable. Each of these claims would
require validation in a positive-sum domain to support more than
analogical inference.

\subsection{Limitations}
Several design choices constrain generalizability of the present
work.
\textit{First, sample size.} All headline correlations are
computed across $n = 5$ random seeds; the Student-$t$ critical
value at $\text{df} = 4$ is 2.776, which requires $|\rtp| > 0.878$
for two-sided significance against zero. The headline Phase 3.1
result has a 95\% bootstrap interval $[-0.32, +0.20]$ that
contains both a fully neutralized correlation and a moderate
residual negative correlation; we cannot statistically separate
``trap broken'' from ``trap attenuated'' at this sample size. The
single highest-leverage follow-up is an $n = 20$ replication of
Phase 3.1 at the same 500-hand horizon as Phase 3, estimated at
${\sim}\$60$ in API spend.
\textit{Second, hand-count asymmetry across phases.} Phases 1, 2,
and 2$^{*}$ play 10\,000 hands per seed; Phase 3 plays 500; Phase
3.1 plays 150. Cross-phase comparisons of per-seed $\rtp$ are
therefore confounded with statistical power. A hand-count-matched
replication is required to interpret the ladder cleanly.
\textit{Third, sampling temperature.} The canonical Phase 3 and
3.1 datasets were generated under SDK-default temperature 1.0;
the released codebase pins temperature 0.0. The headline numbers
reported in this paper are not exactly reproducible from the
current code without manual reversion.
\textit{Fourth, single LLM.} Phase 3 and 3.1 use a single model
family (Anthropic Haiku 4.5). A multi-model replication (e.g.,
Gemini Flash, GPT-4o-mini) is needed to demonstrate that the
attenuation is not model-specific.
\textit{Fifth, the identifiability ``ceiling''} is a stipulated
property of our archetype space (three archetypes have identical
mean parameters), not an emergent finding about reputation
systems in general.
\textit{Sixth, the honesty operationalization} $h(t) = P(\text{bet}
\mid\text{strong})\cdot(1-P(\text{bluff}\mid\text{weak}))$ is one
of several plausible functional forms; the trust--profit
correlation is mechanically anchored on this choice. Reporting
results under at least two alternative honesty definitions is a
useful robustness check.
\textit{Seventh, rebuy economics.} Busted seats are granted a
200-chip rebuy, so the ``final stack'' metric underweights deep
chip losses; \arch{Wall}'s 9.4 mean rebuys per seed in Phase 1
represent 1\,880 chips of true loss not visible in the headline
stack number.
\textit{Eighth, Phase 2$^{*}$ confound.} The unbounded sub-
experiment varies three optimizer parameters simultaneously
(bounds removed, $\delta$ raised $5\times$, evaluation window
shrunk $4\times$). A clean optimization-strength ablation would
change one variable at a time.
\textit{Ninth, Phase 3.1 scaffold confound.} The three scaffolds
(chain-of-thought, per-opponent memory, adaptive notes) were
deployed jointly. A clean main-text ablation establishing the
necessity of each component is the most important missing
experiment.
\textit{Tenth, secondary metrics.} The six secondary metrics
(TEI, CS, OA, NS, SU, TMA) were chosen post hoc and are
unadjusted for multiple comparisons; the family-wise error rate
across them is not controlled.
\textit{Eleventh, zero-sum testbed.} Limit Hold'em is zero-sum
among players; eBay marketplaces, social networks, and credit
systems are positive-sum over trades. Direct cross-domain
inference is rhetorical, not warranted.

\section{Conclusion}
% =============================================================================

We have presented a controlled simulation study of trust dynamics
in 8-player Limit Texas Hold'em. Across four agent architectures,
the Pearson correlation between agent trust score and final stack
falls along the ladder $-0.752 \to -0.637 \to -0.510 \to -0.094$,
with each step accompanied by a confidence interval whose width
exceeds the size of any single inter-phase $\Delta\rtp$. We
characterize the rank ordering of the ladder as the robust finding
and the absolute size of each step as a preliminary estimate
requiring replication. An unbounded hill-climbing sub-experiment
with $11\times$ greater parameter drift produces $\Delta\rtp =
+0.028$ from bounded Phase 2 and yields a population that diverges
rather than converges in 36-dimensional parameter space---
preliminary evidence against the hypothesis that the trap is a
bound-box artifact, though Phase 2$^{*}$ varies three optimizer
parameters at once and does not cleanly isolate the bounds.

Three follow-up experiments are the most important next steps.
\textit{First}, an $n = 20$ Phase 3.1 replication at 500 hands per
seed (matching Phase 3) is the highest-leverage single experiment:
it would tighten the confidence interval on the Phase 3.1 result by
roughly a factor of two and distinguish ``trap attenuated'' from
``trap broken''; estimated cost ${\sim}\$60$ in API spend.
\textit{Second}, a clean ablation of the three Phase 3.1 scaffolds
(chain-of-thought, per-opponent memory, adaptive notes) is required
to support the claim that all three are necessary.
\textit{Third}, a temperature-pinned re-run of Phase 3 and Phase
3.1 at $T = 0.0$ would resolve the open reproducibility caveat.
Beyond these three, a multi-LLM replication, a no-limit Hold'em
extension, and an experimental varying of inter-archetype distance
would each strengthen the present findings.
