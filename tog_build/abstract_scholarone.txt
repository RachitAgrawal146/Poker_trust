Reputation systems that infer trustworthiness from observed behavior are
widespread in online marketplaces, credit scoring, and social platforms; the
transparency paradox -- that a perfectly visible cooperative record earns lower
returns than a less legible one -- is a hypothesis, not an established fact. We
test it in a controlled 8-player fixed-limit Texas Hold'em simulation where
every agent maintains a categorical posterior over every other agent's
archetype, updated after each observed action. Holding game and trust model
fixed, we vary only the agents' minds across four architectures: frozen
rule-based archetypes, bounded online hill-climbing, large-language-model
personality role-players (Claude Haiku 4.5), and the same model augmented with
chain-of-thought, per-opponent memory, and self-updated strategy notes. Across
five random seeds per condition (twenty, at temperature 0, for the final
architecture), the negative Pearson correlation between trust and final stack
attenuates monotonically along the ladder r_tp = -0.752, -0.637, -0.510, +0.062
(SDs across seeds 0.073, 0.125, 0.268, 0.378); an unbounded hill-climbing check
suggests the trap is not an artifact of the archetype bound boxes. The final
architecture's 95% bootstrap interval [-0.10, +0.22] is centered on zero and
excludes every earlier architecture. Hand counts fall from 10,000 per seed in
the rule-based phases to 500 and 150 in the LLM phases, so the ladder mixes
capability with statistical power. This is preliminary evidence that
observation-based reputation rewards exploitation under simple policies and that
reasoning about the reputation system attenuates the effect; whether that
attenuation is structural is left to a hand-count-matched replication.
